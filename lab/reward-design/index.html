Designing rewards for a small learning agent

Consider a learning agent whose task is to reach a goal while avoiding hazards along the way. A reasonable starting point would be to reward it for reaching the goal, penalize encounters with hazards, and give each step a small cost so that it has a reason to finish promptly. These choices seem fairly straightforward, though the behavior they encourage depends on how the rewards work together. If we also reward the agent for remaining active, for example, we need to consider whether finishing the task is still the most attractive outcome.

This article works through that question using a small navigation environment and tabular Q-learning. The environment is simple enough that we can follow the agent's decisions, change a reward, and look at how its learned behavior changes. The interactive figure runs inside the page, allowing us to compare those choices directly while keeping the world and learning procedure the same.

The task and its environment

The world is an 8 × 6 grid with 48 cells. The agent starts at S in the lower left and is shown as a cyan circle labeled A. Its destination is the magenta diamond labeled G in the upper right. It can choose one of four actions at each step: move up, right, down, or left. Walls, shown as light gray crossed squares, block movement, while magenta triangles mark hazards that the agent can enter at a cost.

With the default settings, an ordinary step gives a reward of $-0.2$. Entering a hazard adds a penalty of $-8$, bringing that step's reward to $-8.2$. Reaching the goal gives $+20$ and ends the episode; this goal reward replaces the ordinary step reward on that transition. An attempt to move into a wall or beyond the grid leaves the agent in place, but still counts as a step and receives the reward associated with the resulting cell. These rules make it possible to distinguish movement that helps complete the task from movement that merely uses up more of the episode.

Each episode begins at the same starting cell and stops either when the agent reaches the goal or after 96 steps. The latter outcome is reported as a timeout. This bound keeps each training episode and policy replay short enough to inspect, including cases where the agent repeatedly chooses an action that leaves it in the same place.

The Q-learning update

For context, the state $s$ in this experiment is the agent's current cell, and an action $a$ is one of its four movement choices. The agent keeps a table of values $Q(s,a)$ that estimate the discounted reward it can obtain by taking an action and then continuing from the next state. After observing a reward $r$ and a resulting state $s'$, it updates the entry for the action it just took:

Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha \left[ r \; + \; \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') \; - \; Q(s, a) \right]

The learning rate $\alpha=0.25$ determines how much of the difference between the current estimate and the new target is applied. The discount $\gamma=0.95$ reduces the contribution of rewards that lie further ahead, while $\max_{a'}Q(s',a')$ supplies the best current estimate available from the next cell. Reaching the goal sets that future-value term to zero, since the task has ended. The 96-step cap is handled as a timeout; the final ordinary transition still uses the next cell's estimate before the episode stops.

For example, suppose the agent takes an action into the goal while that action's table entry is still zero. The reward is 20 and there is no future contribution, so the update gives $Q(s,a)=0.25\times20=5$. This entry belongs to the action taken from the cell immediately before the goal. When another transition enters that predecessor cell, its target can include the learned value of 5. Through repeated transitions, information about reaching the goal can therefore influence choices further back along a route. Watkins and Dayan examine the convergence of this procedure under conditions on sampling and learning rates.

During training, the agent also needs opportunities to try actions whose values it has not yet learned. It uses an $\varepsilon$-greedy rule, choosing a random action with probability $\varepsilon$ and otherwise choosing the action with the highest current value. Exploration starts at $0.5$ and decreases toward a floor of $0.08$ as episodes accumulate.Hyperparameters: learning rate $\alpha = 0.25$, discount $\gamma = 0.95$, exploration $\varepsilon = 0.5$ decaying toward $0.08$. Ties in equal action values break by action order. The Watch policy and Step controls instead use the highest-valued action without exploration or updates. Before training, the table contains equal values and ties follow a fixed action order, so a replay can repeatedly choose an unhelpful action until it times out. Comparing that replay with one after training gives us a concrete way to see what the updates have changed.

Comparing the reward settings

Start by launching the figure with its default settings and pressing Train agent. Each press adds 500 training episodes. Once the batch finishes, use Watch policy to follow the learned route. The step cost gives the agent an incentive to finish promptly, while the larger hazard penalty makes a detour worthwhile when it avoids those cells. Look at the route, whether it reaches the goal, and how many steps it takes, alongside the reported return.

Next, consider a setting where activity itself is rewarded. Set the step reward to $+1$, lower the goal reward to $5$, and train again. The agent can now collect rewards while remaining away from the goal, whereas arriving there ends that opportunity. This gives it a reason to prefer continued activity, including attempts to move into a wall that leave its position unchanged. Inspect whether the learned policy reaches the goal or uses the full step allowance. The balance between the two rewards matters here; changing the sign of the step reward alone does not guarantee a particular learned behavior.

For a third comparison, restore the goal reward to $20$ and the step reward to $-0.2$, then set the hazard penalty to zero. After retraining, examine whether the route passes through a triangle. Entering those cells now has the same immediate reward as entering an ordinary cell, so the agent has no additional reward-based reason to avoid them. If avoiding hazards was part of what we intended, this comparison helps show how that intention depends on an explicit cost in the reward function.

Changing a reward clears the learned table, so each comparison needs a new training batch. Additionally, the displayed returns are ordinary sums of the rewards received, while Q-learning uses the discounted target described above. Looking at return together with the outcome and route helps us understand what a high number represents, especially when changing the reward function changes the meaning of the score itself.

Execution and accessibility

The figure downloads its experiment code after an explicit launch and runs the learning procedure in a Web Worker. This keeps the computation separate from the page's interface work, allowing the reader to continue scrolling and reading during training. Closing the experiment returns to the launch control, while session identifiers keep messages from an earlier reset from affecting the current run. If the worker fails, a retry control is shown alongside the article.

The controls support keyboard use, and a textual summary gives the agent's position, outcome, and metrics alongside the canvas. Symbols distinguish the agent, goal, hazards, and walls in addition to their colors. Reduced-motion preferences remove decorative animation, and the Pause and Step controls let the reader inspect the policy one action at a time. Together, these choices provide several ways to follow the experiment according to how the reader wants to interact with it.

Interpreting the experiment

Working with a small, fixed world makes it easier to connect a reward choice to the transitions and updates that follow. The implementation uses reproducible seeds, with tests covering movement rules, reward calculations, value updates, and the browser's training and replay controls. Its fixed learning rate and finite training batches still leave a distinction between observing a useful policy and establishing theoretical convergence. The state also contains only the current cell, so the agent cannot choose differently according to how many steps remain before a timeout.

These details help define what we can take away from the comparisons. We can inspect how the selected rewards influence this agent's route, and how those incentives relate to the task we had in mind. Extending that observation to a larger environment or a neural agent would require further experiments. The useful question to carry forward is whether the reward function gives an agent a reason to pursue each part of the intended outcome, including the constraints that seem obvious to the person designing the task.
