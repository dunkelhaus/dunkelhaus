Rebuilding a cancer-classification experiment

Different cancers can have different patterns of DNA mutations. This raises a fairly natural question: if we describe those patterns to a model, how much can it tell us about the cancer they came from? In this project, I looked at that question for lung adenocarcinoma and melanoma, using eight experiments that change what a training example contains. Some use one mutation at a time, while others summarize a tumor or use a much larger matrix of sample features.

Five of these experiments classified all 22 final test tumors correctly. On its own, though, that is not enough to tell us whether we have a useful cancer classifier. We first need to understand how the data were represented, what the models could learn from them, and what was actually held out during evaluation. The sample matrix also has a considerable amount of missing data whose meaning is not yet verified. I will work through those choices before looking at the results, since they determine what we can reasonably take away from them.

Background: mutation signatures and the classification problem

A somatic mutation is a change acquired by a cell, rather than one inherited from a parent. Mutations can arise through several processes, including errors during DNA replication and exposure to mutagens. Most do not cause the cancer themselves, but the distribution of mutations can still tell us something about the processes a tumor has been exposed to. This is the motivation behind studying mutational signatures: recurring patterns over substitution types and their surrounding DNA sequence.

For example, C→T means that a cytosine was replaced by a thymine. The bases immediately before and after that position provide its local context. We can write A[C→T]G for a C→T substitution with A on the 5′ side and G on the 3′ side. The prime notation describes direction along the DNA strand; it does not mean the fifth and third positions away from the mutation. By representing substitutions with C or T as the original base, the complementary-strand descriptions reduce to six substitution classes. With four possible bases on either side, there are $4\times6\times4=96$ immediate-context classes.

From pmsignature to a supervised problem

Shiraishi and colleagues introduced pmsignature as a probabilistic model for inferring mutation signatures. A tumor can contain mutations from several signatures, with its own mixture of those signatures. Within each signature, the model treats the mutation features as independent. That assumption is useful because it reduces the number of parameters needed to describe a signature, even when additional flanking positions are included.

P(b,s,c\mid t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{tk}\,\phi_{k,5}(b)\,\phi_{k,S}(s)\,\phi_{k,3}(c)

Here $t$ identifies a tumor; $b,s,c$ are the upstream base, substitution, and downstream base; and $k$ indexes one of $K$ latent signatures. The weights $\pi_{tk}$ sum to one for each tumor. Each $\phi$ is a categorical probability distribution for one feature within a signature. For immediate context alone, the separate distributions need $(4-1)+(6-1)+(4-1)=11$ free parameters per signature, compared with $96-1=95$ for an unrestricted joint distribution. Independence is conditional on a signature; mixing signatures can still produce associations between features in a tumor.

For this project, I took the representation question in a supervised direction by using the supplied cancer type as the target: lung adenocarcinoma is $y=0$, and melanoma is $y=1$. This lets us compare marginal and joint mutation features through their classification results. It also changes the question being answered; pmsignature estimates latent signatures and tumor-specific mixture weights, whereas these classifiers learn a relationship between the prepared features and an existing label. The source filenames include the word independent, but establishing patient independence would require a patient mapping in addition to that name.

Where the retained data come from

This work reconstructs the local experiments from an older cancer-therapy proof of concept. That project's stated goal was to compare tumor DNA in the hope of informing treatment. The files retained here support a narrower question: predicting their supplied cancer labels. They contain neither treatment assignments nor outcomes, so this report cannot test the original treatment goal.

Two tab-separated exports are available: fm_mutations_independent.tab and fm_sample_independent.tab. Their hashes and schemas can be verified, but the retained material does not establish a study accession, sequencing and variant-calling protocol, patient mapping, or a complete dictionary for the sample features. The pmsignature paper provides the methodological background; it does not, by itself, verify the provenance of these particular exports. I therefore refer to 110 tumor identifiers, rather than claiming 110 independently recruited patients.

Several rows can have the same 14 values. In fact, the 88 development tumors contain all 96 possible valid contexts, despite contributing millions of mutation rows. For example, the two repeated rows in the preview could describe mutations at different genomic positions, but those positions are absent from this file, leaving us unable to check whether a variant was duplicated. Removing repeated vectors would remove mutation counts that the tumor representations are intended to preserve.

Mutation burden and composition are different signals

There are 42 lung and 68 melanoma identifiers in the full cohort. For the following exploratory summaries I use only the saved development set: 34 lung and 54 melanoma tumors. This audit was added after the experiments to explain their fixed inputs; it did not select features or change a model using the final test set.

The median retained count is 21,599 for lung and 22,143 for melanoma, but the upper quartiles differ: about 21,764 and 44,582. The maximum counts are 65,229 and 67,351. This matters because a count vector carries both composition and total burden. Dividing by a tumor's own retained count removes that explicit scale, letting us ask whether its relative mutation composition is still useful.

C→T makes up about 27.37% of a lung tumor's mutations on average in this development set, compared with 54.92% for melanoma. C→A goes in the other direction, at 21.27% and 9.09%. These differences give a concrete reason to try the representation, since the class averages contain information that a classifier might use. Connecting that information to the process behind an individual tumor's mutations would require further biological analysis, while checking whether the differences persist would require another cohort.

Missing values are the main data-quality question

In the 88 development rows, 122,205,528 of 131,970,344 cells are NA. The 9,764,816 observed numeric values contain no zeros; their minimum and median are both 1, the 95th percentile is 2, and the maximum is 106,127. This leaves us with a small observed portion made up mostly of ones and some much larger values, alongside a much larger portion whose interpretation depends on what the export means by NA.

Missingness ranges from 87.69% to 97.55% per development tumor, with a median of 92.36%. There are 73,033 columns with no observed development value at all. The absence of numeric zeros makes the distinction between “not observed” and “absent” particularly important. It is consistent with several possible export conventions, but does not identify which one was used. Replacing NA with zero or reconstructing it with a latent model makes a substantive assumption either way.

The recorded runs chose the latter: preserve every numeric observation, treat NA as unknown, and fit an imputer using training rows only. I explain that algorithm below, but its result should be read conditionally. Until the export is documented, even a high score cannot resolve whether we completed missing measurements or assigned values to entries that should have represented absence.

Feature construction, retention, and training examples

The experiments compare predefined representations, retaining the coordinates specified for each one. They do so without a fitted feature-selection stage, such as a top-ranked gene list, univariate label filter, or recursive elimination. This distinction matters when following the pipelines, since constructing a representation, filling missing entries, and choosing a subset of columns are different operations.

From one mutation to a tumor representation

Let $t$ index a tumor and $r$ index one of its $n_t$ retained mutations. Write $b_{tr}$ for the upstream base, $s_{tr}$ for substitution type, and $c_{tr}$ for the downstream base. The base alphabet is A, C, G, T; substitution order is C→A, C→G, C→T, T→A, T→C, T→G. The indicator $\mathbf{1}\{E\}$ below means one if event $E$ is true and zero otherwise.

x_{tr}=[\mathrm{onehot}_4(b_{tr}),\mathrm{onehot}_6(s_{tr}),\mathrm{onehot}_4(c_{tr})]\in\{0,1\}^{14}

M1 uses the vector for one mutation. To move to a tumor-level example, M2 sums each indicator over that tumor's mutations, keeping the three marginal distributions. M3 counts complete upstream–substitution–downstream combinations instead. The distinction becomes useful when two tumors have similar marginal counts but arrange those features into different contexts; M3 retains those associations, while M2 loses them during aggregation.

Evaluation protocol and how to read the figures

For the seven tumor-level experiments, I used the same seeded, shuffled, stratified split. The development portion supplies five folds for fitting and validation, while the final portion is evaluated once the development procedure is complete. Sharing these assignments makes the representation comparisons easier to interpret, since the models are evaluated on the same tumors. M1 uses mutation-row assignments, which gives it a different evaluation population and requires its results to be considered separately.

In fold $k$, the other four development folds supply the rows used to fit the scaler, any applicable imputer and MDS model, and the classifier's parameters. The remaining fold supplies validation predictions; collecting them across the five fits gives one out-of-fold (OOF) prediction for each development identity. For the neural models, validation loss also selects the best epoch. Keeping final-test rows outside this process prevents their statistics and labels from influencing rank, transforms, hyperparameters, or stopping duration, which is important when a pipeline has several fitted preprocessing stages.

\hat p_i^{\mathrm{OOF}}=f_{-k(i)}(x_i),\qquad e_{\mathrm{final}}=\mathrm{round}\bigl(\mathrm{median}(e_1^*,\ldots,e_5^*)\bigr)

Here $k(i)$ is row $i$'s validation fold. The notation $f_{-k(i)}$ refers to the exclusion of that fold from preprocessing fits and weight updates. Its labels still select the best epoch $e_k^*$, making the selection decision dependent on the validation fold. Neural OOF predictions are therefore validation-selected and can be optimistic relative to an untouched evaluation of the entire selection procedure. Estimating that procedure through nested cross-validation would require an additional held-out level, which these runs do not have. A new neural model is then fitted on all development data for the fixed duration $e_{\mathrm{final}}$. When summarizing development results, pooling these predictions gives a different calculation from averaging the five fold scores. In particular, pooled AUROC compares probabilities produced by different fitted models. The reported mean and standard deviation describe variation across these five folds; estimating a confidence interval would require additional statistical assumptions.

Every model estimates $p_i=P(y_i=1\mid x_i)$, with melanoma positive. The preselected decision rule is $\hat y_i=\mathbf{1}\{p_i\geq0.5\}$. For melanoma, true positives (TP) are correctly predicted melanomas; false positives (FP) are lung tumors called melanoma. False negatives (FN) are melanomas called lung, and true negatives (TN) are correct lung predictions.

\mathrm{precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\quad\mathrm{recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\quad\mathrm{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}

A ROC curve plots recall, also called true positive rate, against false positive rate as the threshold changes. Its area, AUROC, summarizes how well the probabilities rank the two classes across thresholds. A precision–recall (PR) curve looks at the same predictions through the precision cost of recovering more melanomas; its no-skill reference is the fraction of positive examples in that partition. By comparison, a confusion matrix fixes the threshold at 0.5 and shows exactly which decisions it produces. Reading these views together helps distinguish a ranking problem from a problem with the chosen decision threshold. Each experiment below includes both evaluations, followed by fold variation and training history, with full-size images available through their links.

What the neural networks learn

The seven neural experiments use the same kind of fully connected network, with 16 units in the first hidden layer and 8 in the second. Each unit combines the values from the preceding layer through learned weights, adds a bias, and applies an activation function. Keeping these hidden widths fixed makes it easier to follow what changes when we give the network a different representation of a tumor.

From a feature vector to a probability

Let $\tilde x_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ be a completed input row; for the mutation experiments, the original representation is already complete. Each coordinate is standardized using the mean $\bar x_j$ and population standard deviation $s_j$ of the current training rows. A constant column uses scale one, so it can remain in the vector without division by zero:

z_{ij}=\frac{\tilde x_{ij}-\bar x_j}{s_j},\qquad v_j=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\tilde x_{ij}-\bar x_j)^2,\qquad s_j=\begin{cases}\sqrt{v_j},&v_j>0\\1,&v_j=0\end{cases}

The fitted means and scales are then reused for validation and final-test rows. In S2, this standardization precedes MDS; the network's input $z_i$ is the resulting ten-coordinate embedding, which is left on its MDS scale. For the other neural experiments, $z_i$ is the standardized feature vector itself. With column vectors, the forward calculation is:

h_i^{(1)}=\rho(W_1z_i+b_1),\qquad h_i^{(2)}=\rho(W_2h_i^{(1)}+b_2) \rho(u)=\max(0,u),\qquad p_i=\sigma(w_3^{\mathsf T}h_i^{(2)}+b_3),\qquad\sigma(a)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-a}}

For example, a hidden unit with a negative weighted sum outputs zero after ReLU, while a positive sum passes through unchanged. The second layer combines those responses, allowing the classifier to use interactions between input features. The sigmoid maps the final score into the interval from zero to one; at the fixed decision threshold, $p_i\geq0.5$ gives a melanoma prediction. Its interpretation as a well-calibrated probability would require a separate calibration check, which these experiments did not include.

The objective used during training

Binary cross-entropy gives a penalty for assigning a low probability to the observed class. If $\mathcal B$ is a training batch, the implemented neural objective adds an L2 penalty to the two hidden weight matrices:

\mathcal L_{\mathcal B}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal B|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal B}\left[y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\right]+\lambda\left(\lVert W_1\rVert_F^2+\lVert W_2\rVert_F^2\right)

Here $\theta$ contains all weights and biases, $\lVert W\rVert_F^2$ is the sum of squared matrix entries, and $\lambda=10^{-4}$. For a melanoma example, the data-loss term is $-\log p_i$; increasing a correct probability from 0.5 to 0.9 reduces that term from about 0.693 to 0.105. The added penalty discourages large hidden-layer weights. The code leaves biases and the output weights unpenalized, which matters when specifying the actual objective rather than referring to L2 regularization generally.

Adam uses the batch gradients to update the parameters, with learning rate 0.001. During a development fold, the same objective is evaluated on its validation rows without parameter updates. The recorded val_loss therefore includes the hidden-kernel penalty as well as cross-entropy. Training stops after ten epochs without an improvement, and the best weights are restored. The earlier evaluation section explains how this validation choice determines the fixed duration of the final fit.

Input width also changes model capacity

A shared hidden architecture does not give every experiment the same number of parameters. Counting weights and biases gives $16(d+1)+8(16+1)+(8+1)=16d+161$. In particular, S1's full-width first layer produces nearly 24 million parameters, despite having only 88 development tumors available for its final fit. This gives us a reason to consider sensitivity to model capacity when reading its results, though a controlled comparison would be needed to isolate that effect.

M1 · Can one mutation predict the tumor label?

Setup and run

M1 asks how informative a single mutation's local context is. The input is the 14-indicator vector defined above, standardized with training-only means and scales. Each mutation inherits its tumor's supplied label. With the tumor identifier excluded from the input, the network receives each mutation row on its own. It therefore has no tumor histogram from which to aggregate that tumor's evidence directly.

The split keeps row identities disjoint, while allowing a tumor to contribute different mutations to both partitions. As a result, the evaluation asks how well the model predicts another mutation row under this shared-tumor setting. Testing generalization to a new tumor would require holding out all of its rows together. The development folds selected a final training duration of 46 epochs, with their individual stopping records shown in the table below.

Interpretation

The OOF and final ROC curves are fairly close, which suggests consistent performance across these row partitions. Looking at the class metrics, however, makes the limitation of the result clearer: the model recovers many more melanoma mutations than lung mutations, and calls roughly two thirds of lung mutation rows melanoma at the fixed threshold. I would therefore describe the result as limited discrimination from a single local context. The millions of rows let us measure those row-level rates precisely within the file, while their shared tumor origins still limit what we can infer about new patients or compare with the tumor-level runs.

M2 · Aggregate the marginal mutation counts

Setup and run

M2 changes the prediction unit to a tumor. It sums the indicators for all retained mutations, preserving how often each upstream base, substitution, and downstream base occurs. For indicator position $j$:

x_{tj}^{\mathrm{M2}}=\sum_{r=1}^{n_t}x_{trj},\qquad\sum_{j\in G}x_{tj}^{\mathrm{M2}}=n_t

$G$ is any one of the three indicator groups. Each group's total is the tumor's retained mutation count, so M2 retains both composition and burden. The 14 counts are standardized within each training scope and passed directly to the shared neural architecture after scaling, keeping the preprocessing limited to this complete 14-coordinate representation. The development-selected final refit duration was 149 epochs; the individual fold stopping records are tabulated below.

Setup and run

The shared missing-data model for S0–S2

The sample experiments use the much wider source matrix, retaining every original feature. Moving from the 14- or 96-bin mutation histograms to this matrix also introduces a missing-data problem, since most cells are NA. How those entries are completed therefore affects what the classifiers receive. S0–S2 share each fitted imputer and scaler only when the exact training scope matches.

Initialize from observed training values

The purpose of the imputer is to estimate an unknown feature using the relationships available in the observed training data. Let $i$ index a training tumor and $j$ a feature, and let $\Omega$ contain the positions with numeric observations. For a column with at least one observation, its initial offset is:

n_j=|\{i:(i,j)\in\Omega\}|,\qquad\mu_j=\frac{1}{n_j}\sum_{i:(i,j)\in\Omega}x_{ij}\quad(n_j>0)

These means remain fixed throughout a fit. A column with $n_j=0$ uses the global mean of the observed training entries, following the fallback described below. To work in centered coordinates, subtract $\mu_j$ from each observed value and set the missing positions to zero:

C_{ij}^{(0)}=\begin{cases}x_{ij}-\mu_j,&(i,j)\in\Omega\\0,&(i,j)\in\Omega^c\end{cases}

A centered zero at a missing position corresponds to filling that position with its training column mean. It gives the first decomposition a complete matrix to work with. As the algorithm proceeds, the observed entries provide fixed constraints and only these provisional missing values are revised.

Alternate the low-rank fit and the missing-value update

At iteration $q$, take a singular value decomposition of the current centered matrix. Retaining its $m$ largest singular values gives a rank-$m$ approximation $R_m^{(q)}$:

C^{(q)}=U^{(q)}\Sigma^{(q)}(Q^{(q)})^{\mathsf T},\qquad R_m^{(q)}=U_m^{(q)}\Sigma_m^{(q)}(Q_m^{(q)})^{\mathsf T}

The diagonal entries of $\Sigma_m$ are the retained singular values; $U_m$ and $Q_m$ contain the corresponding left and right singular vectors. In the earlier notation, $A=U_m\Sigma_m$ contains the row coefficients and $V=Q_m$ contains the feature loadings. Their product approximates the centered matrix, so an individual reconstructed value has the form $\mu_j+a_i^{\mathsf T}v_j$.

Now let $P_\Omega(B)$ keep the entries of a matrix $B$ at observed positions and set the remaining positions to zero; $P_{\Omega^c}$ does the corresponding operation for missing positions. The update is:

C^{(q+1)}=P_\Omega(X-\mathbf1\mu^{\mathsf T})+P_{\Omega^c}(R_m^{(q)}),\qquad R_m^{(q+1)}=\mathrm{SVD}_m(C^{(q+1)})

The first term keeps every observed centered value unchanged, while the second supplies estimates where values are missing. The updated matrix is decomposed again, allowing those estimates to affect the next set of factors. This alternating procedure is what distinguishes the fit from a single mean fill followed by one SVD. The expression involving $X$ reads only observed positions, so an unknown entry is never used as an arithmetic value.

Measure both reconstruction error and changing estimates

After each update and re-estimation, the implementation records two diagnostics. The first compares the new low-rank fit with observed centered values. The second measures how much the missing part changed relative to its previous size:

E_{q+1}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\Omega}\left[(R_m^{(q+1)})_{ij}-(x_{ij}-\mu_j)\right]^2} \delta_{q+1}=\frac{\lVert P_{\Omega^c}(C^{(q+1)}-C^{(q)})\rVert_F}{\max(\lVert P_{\Omega^c}(C^{(q)})\rVert_F,\epsilon)}

Here $\epsilon$ is float64 machine epsilon, used to guard the zero norm at initialization. Restoring observations makes the completed matrix exact at those positions; $E$ can still be positive because it measures the separate low-rank approximation. The loop stops when $\delta$ is below $10^{-4}$, when relative improvement in $E$ is at most $10^{-4}$ for five consecutive iterations, or after 100 updates. The latter two outcomes carry warnings, since they can leave the missing estimates changing above the convergence tolerance.

At termination, the fit keeps the last completed matrix for training and the newly re-estimated factors for later projection. With early termination, its missing values can therefore differ from one more update using those factors. Additionally, restoring observations means the completed matrix can have rank greater than $m$, even though the reconstruction used to update missing entries has rank at most $m$.

Select rank by reconstructing held-out training entries

In each training scope, three seeded repeats hide 10% of eligible observed entries while retaining row and column support. Every candidate rank uses the same masks. Mean hidden-entry RMSE chooses the rank, with lower rank breaking a tie; the winner is refitted on the original observations. The rank comparison is confined to training tumors, with validation and final-test tumors kept outside it. It therefore selects for reconstructing entries within the available training data; how useful that completion is for predicting cancer labels remains a question for classifier evaluation.

\mathrm{RMSE}_{m,r}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|H_r|}\sum_{(i,j)\in H_r}(x_{ij}-\tilde x_{ij}^{(m,r)})^2},\qquad m^*=\mathrm{arg\,min}_m\frac13\sum_{r=1}^{3}\mathrm{RMSE}_{m,r}

$H_r$ contains the observed entries hidden in repeat $r$, and $\tilde x^{(m,r)}$ is the completion fitted with those entries withheld. In particular, each repeat recomputes its offsets from the remaining observations; the hidden values contribute only to this error score. A smaller rank wins a tie. The chosen rank is then refitted with all original training observations restored.

Transform a new tumor without refitting the basis

For a held-out tumor, only its own observed entries fit row coefficients against the frozen training offsets and loadings. If $O_i$ denotes its usable observed feature positions:

a_i^*=\mathrm{arg\,min}_a\sum_{j\in O_i}(x_{ij}-\mu_j-v_j^{\mathsf T}a)^2

The minimum-norm least-squares solution uses a relative singular-value cutoff of $10^{-6}$. Missing values then use $\mu_j+v_j^{\mathsf T}a_i^*$; observed values remain unchanged. One held-out tumor cannot alter the basis or another held-out tumor's completion. Columns never observed in training use the global observed training mean and zero loadings; unsupported rows use zero coefficients. The final training scope has 73,033 unsupported columns. These explicit fallbacks preserve the feature positions in the matrix, although an unsupported column still lacks biological evidence from the training data. Any negative completions remain as calculated and are recorded without clipping. Training-fitted standardization follows imputation.

S0 · A linear classifier on the full sample matrix

Setup and run

S0 uses every imputed, standardized sample feature. L2 logistic regression provides a linear log-odds baseline before introducing another neural network or a dimension reduction. Let $z_i$ be the completed, scaled training row and $\sigma$ the sigmoid:

p_i=\sigma(w^{\mathsf T}z_i+b),\quad\min_{w,b}\left[-\frac1n\sum_i\bigl(y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\bigr)+\frac{\lVert w\rVert_2^2}{2Cn}\right]

With $C=1$, the penalty controls the size of $w$ while leaving the intercept $b$ unpenalized. The GPU implementation uses float64 L-BFGS for this objective, with numerical checks against the CPU library solver. Each fold starts from fresh parameters and uses optimizer convergence to determine its duration. In the final fit, the optimizer converged after 80 iterations, with gradient infinity norm approximately $9.0\times10^{-8}$, below the $10^{-7}$ tolerance. This describes the logistic fit specifically; the earlier imputer still carries its recorded plateau warning.

S2 · Ten-dimensional classical MDS before the network

Setup and run

S2 asks whether a low-dimensional account of the completed sample geometry is useful to the classifier. It uses the same full-width imputation and scaling, fits Euclidean classical multidimensional scaling (MDS) inside the training scope, and sends ten coordinates to the neural network. The ten-coordinate width is fixed independently of final-test performance, and the fitted transform uses classical MDS throughout.

For $n$ training rows, let $D^{(2)}$ be their $n\times n$ squared-distance matrix. $H=I-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}/n$ subtracts the mean. Double centering converts distances to a centered Gram matrix:

B=-\tfrac12 H D^{(2)}H=V\Lambda V^{\mathsf T},\qquad Z_{10}=V_{10}\Lambda_{10}^{1/2}

Columns of $V_{10}$ are the ten leading eigenvectors; the diagonal of $\Lambda_{10}$ contains their positive eigenvalues. $Z_{10}$ therefore has one row per training tumor and ten coordinates. The implementation rejects materially negative spectra and requests beyond the supported positive rank, with no absolute-value repair.

Each new tumor is projected individually against the frozen training reference, keeping it outside any joint embedding with other held-out rows. If $d_j^2$ is its squared distance to training tumor $j$, $r_j$ is training row $j$'s mean squared distance, and $g$ is the training grand mean:

b_j=-\tfrac12(d_j^2-\overline{d^2}-r_j+g),\qquad z_{\mathrm{new}}=b^{\mathsf T}V_{10}\Lambda_{10}^{-1/2}

All reference points, distance means, eigenvectors, and eigenvalues remain fixed, and the coordinates retain their MDS scale without a second standardization. Tests of training self-projection, serialization, and equivalence to Euclidean PCA scores up to sign or rotation check the numerical implementation. Interpreting the comparisons

To me, the most useful mutation comparison is counts versus frequencies on the shared tumor split. The EDA showed variation in burden, and M4 and M5 retain strong results when the model is given relative composition instead of the count scale. The differences among those models are small, however: using joint counts changes one pooled OOF error, and normalizing those joint counts changes one more. I would want to see how those differences behave across other samples before using them to rank the representations, particularly while patient-level independence remains unverified in this cohort.

The biological background provides a reasonable motivation for these differences, since tobacco- and ultraviolet-associated processes can leave different mutation patterns in lung cancers and melanomas. Establishing that connection for these classifiers would require estimating signature exposures and checking alternative explanations, including cohort and technical effects. What the present experiments contribute is a measured relationship between the chosen representations and the supplied labels, which could support that further investigation.

The wide-matrix comparison leaves a different set of questions. S0 succeeds on the final set, S1's stronger OOF result transfers poorly, and S2 is weak in both evaluations, despite their shared completion and scaling. Classifier choice and projection therefore deserve closer attention. Before investing in those comparisons, though, I would prioritize resolving the sample export's NA convention, since it determines what all three pipelines were asked to reconstruct. Their nonconverged imputation diagnostics add a further reason to revisit that preparation stage.
